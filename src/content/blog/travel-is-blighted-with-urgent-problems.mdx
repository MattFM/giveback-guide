The tourism industry has a secret. **It’s in crisis**. It’s trying really hard to pretend that it isn’t, and many within the industry don’t even realise there’s a crisis at all. But there is.

In simple terms, the industry is **inherently unsustainable**. But acknowledging this is barely good enough to even start to unpick the multitude of interconnected and complex problems that it is facing.

In future articles in this series, I’ll be taking a much more balanced approach by focusing on both the problems and solutions in equal measure. In this very first article though, I want to broadly illustrate what we’re up against.

## The problem with travel

Let’s begin by very briefly talking about three problems that are currently facing the travel industry. There are many more and each of the following problems can be spliced into a number of much smaller problems, but we have to start somewhere.

![A sign proclaiming 'Climate Justive Now' is held at a climate protest](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1784628416/climate-justice-now_btpgaq.jpg)

### The climate crisis

According to the World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC), the global travel and tourism industry was responsible for [7.3% of total global greenhouse gas emissions in 2024](https://wttc.org/research/environmental-social).

7.3% seems like a small percentage in isolation, but it’s a gigantic figure when set in context. If travel were a country, it would be the [third-largest emitter in the world](https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu/report_2025), trailing only China, the USA, and India. Insane!

**That makes travel one of the most disproportionately carbon-intensive activities on the planet**. And these figures only include transport emissions, so in reality, it’s much worse.

Current projections by UN Tourism indicate that, without significant policy changes, [transport-related CO₂ emissions from the sector are on track to grow 25%](https://www.untourism.int/sustainable-development/tourism-emissions-climate-change) by 2030 compared to 2016 levels.

### Overtourism and local communities

Overtourism is an umbrella problem, underneath which there are a multitude of individual issues, the most visible of which is **the displacement of locals which changes the very fabric of a location**.

This manifests in two ways: in sheer visitor numbers who swamp the local population, and in the diminishing number of available permanent homes in favour of short-term rentals.

In recent times, the impact of overtourism has been strongly felt in some European cities. In 2024/25, [Barcelona recorded a tourist-to-resident ratio of 10:1](https://barcelonasecreta.com/en/barcelona-the-most-overcrowded-tourist-city-in-the-world/) (for every group of 10 residents, there were 90-100 tourists). Venice meanwhile has seen a [72% decline in local residents since 1951](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1454765/venice-population/?srsltid=AfmBOooSkB_TKETbtX-9GVU-cGkcLDLzQBmTohE7ROJKYU-E8-rZum9R), with many fearing the situation has surpassed the point of no return.

In both of these cities, [locals have taken to the streets in protest](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp8d87l6lp1o) and authorities have reactively [introduced tourist taxes](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/apr/17/venice-tourist-fee-returns-and-will-double-for-last-minute-daytrippers) in an effort to tackle the problem.

### Animal welfare (or the lack thereof)

Animal welfare is one of tourism’s major blind spots.

Many tourists have a genuine love for animals and nature, and will seek out experiences that involve animals, but research shows that **a significant proportion of these venues don’t meet basic welfare or conservation standards**.

A global study conducted by Oxford University’s Wildlife Conservation Research Unit (WildCRU) found that an estimated [75% of wildlife tourist attractions had abuse or conservation concerns](https://www.worldanimalprotection.org/latest/news/our-new-global-study-exposes-shameful-suffering-caused-irresponsible-wildlife-tourism/).

Elephant tourism is particularly problematic, with World Animal Protection reporting that only [5% of captive elephants are living in ”high-welfare, observation-only settings”](https://www.worldanimalprotection.org/latest/press-releases/new-report-finds-that-two-out-of-three-captive-elephants-used-in-tourism-live-in-poor-conditions/).

## We’ve barely scratched the surface

These facts and figures are horrible on their own, but each of these three issues are interconnected and highly complex. And there are so many more issues that I haven’t even mentioned.

**That’s why this series of articles exists** - to look into these problems in more depth and to explore how they can be rectified.

## Don’t just take my word for it

Fixing travel requires a multi-disciplinary team effort. There are a whole host of incredible people all over the world, each focusing on specific challenges but collectively working towards a better travel industry and ultimately, a better planet for us all.

I reached out to a couple of our favourite people and asked them just one question… 

### What problem in travel do you think we’re still not taking seriously enough?

Here's what Emma from [That Travel](https://thattravel.co.uk/) had to say ([Find Emma on Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/that_travel))

> I think the biggest problem in tourism is the way the industry is designed and I don't think we're talking about it nearly enough, as it doesn't seem to have permeated to the masses yet. As someone who's worked in tourism my whole adult life I see it in everything the industry does: the customer is ALWAYS right and is treated like the centre of the universe, often before they've even left the country (with travel agents etc). I have a lot of experience with this and can talk about how I saw it in my own career as well as how I've seen it in tourism... it's really sad.

Lotte Smith from [The Sustainable Travel Guide](https://thesustainabletravelguide.com/) ([Lotte is also on Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/the.sustainable.travel.guide/)) also shared her thoughts on the challenges the tourism industry are facing. 

> One of the main issues that is inhibiting people from slow, sustainable travel is the way 'bigger, better, faster' is ingrained in our society and mindsets. Influencers still share itineraries that cram in as many destinations and activities in as short a time as possible. Similarly, [travellers tend set themselves extreme travel goals](https://www.instagram.com/p/DUXlpmADBdx) such as wanting to visit every single country in the world. To promote sustainable travel, I think we need to shift our mindsets to enjoy more relaxed itineraries at nearby destinations, which is no less fulfilling!

## Our theory for fixing travel

We at [Give Back Guide](https://giveback.guide) think the solution to fixing the tourism industry is **rooted in the concept of regenerative travel**.

In short, regenerative travel is an extension of sustainable travel. It takes the somewhat passive “do no harm” mantra of sustainability and adds a more active “revitalise, restore, renew” element. 

**A regenerative traveller aims to leave the place they’re visiting in a better state than they found it.**

Regenerative travel requires the traveller to adopt a new mindset, one that decentres them and instead places the focus on the destination and the local community/ecosystem within it. When this decentralisation occurs, a traveller is in a better position to think more clearly about the implications of their choices and actions.

Our realisation that regenerative travel is the future of the tourism industry is why we created Give Back Guide. We think, and very much hope, that it is part of the solution.
