Let’s kick this off with a headline stat…  

Across the entire global supply chain, the tourism industry emits [5.2 gigatonnes of CO2-equivalent (Gt CO2-e)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-54582-7) annually (recorded in 2019, so it may actually now be higher).  

To most people, this figure is somewhat meaningless, so let’s put it a different way.  

**If the global tourism industry were a country, it would be the third-largest emitter on the planet.**  

Unbelievably, the tourism industry is responsible for emitting more than the entire European Union (3.1 Gt) and Russia (2.0 Gt) combined and easily beats India (around 3.5 Gt), despite India having over 1.4 billion people (Source: [EDGAR](https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu/report_2025)).  

To paint this in a broader sense, tourism accounts for around [8% of global greenhouse gas emissions](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/science-environment-44005013). Comparatively, the entire global construction industry accounts for roughly [6% of global emissions](https://d-carbonize.eu/blog/ghg-emissions-sectors/).  

This makes travel **one of the most disproportionately carbon-intensive activities** on the planet.  

You may have noted that I started this introduction by mentioning that these estimates are made up by measuring the ‘entire global supply chain’ of the tourism industry (that’s the combined emissions of hotel construction, tourist food consumption, transit, retail shopping etc.). That was deliberate and leads me nicely into the main crux of this article.  

When it comes to discussions about tourism’s relationship with climate, flights are often the main talking point. In many cases, you’d be forgiven for thinking it was the only talking point. I’m here to tell you though that flights are only half the story.

## Tourism’s hidden climate footprint  

It’s very clear why flights are the big talking point when it comes to tourism’s climate impact.   

**Flights are the single most carbon-intensive activity in the travel industry**.  

Estimates suggest that [flights account for at least 40% of the sector’s transport-related emissions](https://www.dandc.eu/en/article/tourists-air-travels-are-not-climate-friendly). Conversely, the single most carbon-efficient mass transit option is trains, accounting for [just 1-1.4% of tourism transport emissions](https://ourworldindata.org/co2-emissions-from-transport).  

It’s important to stress that that’s 40% of _transport-related emissions only_.  

There’s no mistaking that flights have a massive negative impact, but when we look at **ALL** _transport related_ emissions combined, they all equate to only 40% of tourism’s **TOTAL** emissions.  

That leaves us with an unaccounted-for 60%. This is the bit hardly anyone is talking about… 

![A smart high-end hotel room with decorative carpets and dark brown wood finishes](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1789561359/smart-hotel-room_qg9lwe.jpg "Luxury 5-star room generates nearly four times more carbon per night than a budget hotel")

## It's all the other stuff  

When you book a trip, you’re not just booking a seat on a plane and a bed for the night. You’re triggering a series of events, all of which are targeted at providing you with the best possible experience, often **at any cost**.  

The hidden footprint includes things like utilities and services (air conditioning, heating, laundry. etc.), agriculture, imported food and drink, fuel manufacturing, retail manufacturing, and the list goes on and on.  

Let’s look at one example; hotels. According to United Nations World Tourism Organisation data, **only one-third** of a hotel's lifetime footprint comes from running it. The other two-thirds come from "embodied carbon". This is the massive amount of pollution caused by manufacturing concrete, shipping building materials, and constructing the hotel. And every time a hotel remodels, the process repeats.  

What type of hotel you stay in matters. Data from the French environment agency [ADEME](https://www.google.com/url?sa=i&source=web&rct=j&url=https://nosgestesclimat.fr/documentation/logement/vacances/empreinte-hotel-par-nuit&ved=2ahUKEwif0_Gu1vCWAxUDV0EAHa8ED18Qy_kOegYIAAgiEAM&opi=89978449&cd&psig=AOvVaw23cE3Y3j8nofSU7AnGMGo9&ust=1789564758069000) shows that a luxury 5-star room generates nearly four times more carbon per night than a budget hotel.   

There’s a really strong link between this and so-called [‘Tourism Leakage’, which we've written about previously](http://giveback.guide/blog/tourism-leakage/). In short, it’s tourist demand and the tourism industry’s unquestioning willingness to provide, that is contributing to tourism’s massive carbon emissions.  

That’s why where you go in the world and what you do when you get there really does matter. The choices you make can be the difference between generating a massive climate footprint or a minimal one.  

_Incidently, we don't blame tourists for demanding the best possible experience when they're on a trip. The tourism industry has, for several decades, sold tourism by centering the wants and needs of the tourist, whilst barely considering the environmental and social impacts that travel has. We've all been consistently told that the traveller is the most important person on a trip._ [_Give Back Guide_](https://giveback.guide/) _exists to address the imbalance and to get people questioning whether actually, it's the places and the communities that live there that should be everyone's top priority._

## What you can do to minimise your climate impact  

Firstly, it's important to acknowledge again that flights are still the single largest source of carbon in tourism. So, if any other mode of transport is a feasible option to get you to your destination, you should take it. This is especially important for shorter journeys.  

## Getting there  

Many of us default to searching for flights when we have a trip in mind, so considering other options will require both a mindset shift and a little extra time. A minor inconvenience when we're talking about the future of our planet.  

I'll be honest... Alternative options to flying often take longer and frustratingly, can be more expensive. What I'm suggesting though is that you simply take the time to look into the different options and consider them, rather than automatically searching for flights.   

You might be surprised by a great deal and by a negligible time difference. Personally we'd rather pay a bit extra and for the journey to be longer in order to reduce our climate impact and to avoid the stresses and restrictions of budget airlines. Also, taking the slower route can often be more fun and an experience in itself.

### Transfers and connections  

Upon arrival in your destination, there are also choices to be made regarding your onward transport. For this, I asked Lotte from [The Sustainable Travel Guide](https://thesustainabletravelguide.com/) for her thoughts on the options we should be considering.  

"Airport transfers, road trip car rentals, private hire taxis - many travellers default to getting around by car. But why not use available public transport options? These are not only likely to be cheaper, they also allow you to experience your destination in a way that locals do too.  

"Both airports and tourist attractions tend to be well-connected by public transport. Though extensive bus or tram networks in cities can be a bit of a puzzle to navigate, there’s a fun challenge in that! It might even lead you to unexpected highlights. Think of beautiful metro stations, heritage tram rides and bus routes through neighbourhoods you otherwise wouldn’t have known about.   

"So even if you do take the unsustainable flight to your destination, your transport choices don’t end there. Help out the locals and support their public transport!"  

## While you're there  

Next up is what you choose to do while you're in your chosen destination. On a trip, we are constantly having to make choices and we often fall into the trap of choosing the option of least resistance. This is usually the most problematic choice we can make.  

A few things you can do to minimise your climate impact are:  

* **Avoiding buffets.** A UN Environment Programme report found that guests waste twice as much food at a breakfast buffet compared to a made-to-order meal. Food production and food haulage is a huge emissions contributor. When food then goes to waste, it means all of that additional carbon was released for no good reason.  
* **Switch everything off.** This one is obvious. Switch off the AC and all the lights when you leave your accommodation.  
* **Avoid motorised excursions.** Switch to human-powered activities like kayaking and cycling.  

Of course, these only really scratch the surface, but they are still helpful.  

One other thing you can do is to adjust your mindset. The tourism industry has, for a long time, gone to great lengths to convince us that the individual traveller is the most important person in tourism. At Give Back Guide, we want to challenge that narrative. We think the destination (and the communities, animals and ecosystems within it) is the most important thing. After all, without sustainable and functioning destinations, tourism can’t exist.  

We've [already written about a few of things](http://giveback.guide/blog/tourism-leakage/) you can do to decentre yourself when it comes to travel. We’re confident that this mindset shift has multiple positive outcomes, for both destinations and the traveller. When you change how you view travel, you begin to consider how your choices and actions impact the places you visit. When this happens, you naturally begin to drift towards activities, excursions and accommodation options that have a positive impact and those tend to be more routed in local community enterprise and initiatives.  

In a nutshell, our advice is to make your travel plans more locally orientated. Buy local, seasonal food and drink, stay in independently run accommodation run by locals and engage in experiences that benefit local people, the local ecosystem and local wildlife.
