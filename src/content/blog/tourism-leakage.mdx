Wouldn't it be great if every single penny or cent you spent in a destination stayed in that destination? Wouldn’t it also be great if local communities and ecosystems enjoyed the financial benefits of tourism?
It would be natural to assume that, as a tourist, your travel money (at least a majority of it) went into the pockets of local people and was spent on improving local services.

For many places around the world (particularly, but not exclusively, countries that are commonly referred to using the highly problematic term ‘developing’), that isn’t happening.

In this edition of the newsletter, we’re looking at **Tourism Leakage**, the term given to this phenomenon. We’ll look at why it happens, what it looks like in reality and what you as a tourist can do to reduce its effects.

## What is Tourism Leakage?

Tourism leakage occurs when revenue generated by tourism leaves the place of origin and instead ‘leaks’ to another beneficiary. Typically, this is most often felt in ‘developing’ nations where money drains out of the local economy and ends up in a ‘developed’ nation instead.

This has the obvious result in local communities losing out financially and not benefiting from the money that they played a significant role in helping to make.

There are two types of tourism leakage:

- **Import leakage:** Occurs when destinations are unable to provide the sufficient resources to support the demands of tourists (e.g. food, equipment and various other goods).
- **Export leakage:** When profits from companies such as multinational hotel chains and airlines are sent back to other countries rather than being locally invested.

## How bad is it?

Finding recent statistical information that illustrates the global impact of tourism leakage is surprisingly difficult.

The most recent and most reputable source is a [2014 report from the UN World Tourism Organisation](https://www.e-unwto.org/doi/epdf/10.18111/9789284416622), who themselves quote the [UN Environment Programme](https://www.unep.org/).

The report paints a grim picture.

“Of each USD 100 spent on a vacation tour by a tourist from a developed country, only around USD 5 actually stays in a developing destination’s economy.”

UNEP cites several studies that collectively approximate “tourism leakage to be up to 40% in India, 70% in Thailand, and 80% in Caribbean countries due to factors such as foreign-owned operators, airlines, hotels, and imported food and products.”

To get a better idea of how tourism leakage is affecting destinations, we need to focus in on specific places. Here are three examples:

![A beautifully ornate scene depicting a temple in Thailand](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1784635218/thailand-temple_nkitjc.jpg)

### Thailand

Some of the worst tourism leakage on the planet occurs in Thailand, where it is estimated that the combined import and export leakage amounts to around 70%. 

To put it another way, for every $100 spent by international tourists in Thailand, a staggering $70 leaves the country.

In 2025, Thailand [welcomed 32.9 million arrivals](https://www.visitthailandtoday.com/thailand-tourism-statistics), generating around $45 billion (USD). Despite some year-to-year fluctuation, these are not small figures and demonstrates just how much money is draining away from Thailand’s economy.

![A white bus is parked alongside a shoreline with the fronds of palm trees above.](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1784635299/jamaica-bus-ocean_jvt6me.jpg)

### Jamaica

Jamaica’s economy relies heavily on tourism. Even in 2020, during the Covid-19 pandemic, [tourism accounted for 30% of the country’s GDP](https://www.preventionweb.net/media/102964).

Leakage is [estimated to be at around 60%](https://jis.gov.jm/govt-pushing-for-greater-retention-of-tourism-earnings-in-jamaica/). The government are in the process of implementing policies to help improve financial retention.

A significant cause of leakage in Jamaica is the demand for all-inclusive resorts, which are often run by multi-national companies. Guests at these resorts often bring with them certain service and facility expectations which the local infrastructure and supply chains are unable to fulfil. This results in significant import leakage.

![A sandy beach full of sunbathers in The Gambia](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1784635405/beach-gambia_jqtbm1.jpg)

### The Gambia

Another example of a country where tourism is so important, but where international hotels, booking platforms and airlines are letting it down.

The [leakage of tourism income in The Gambia is 70%](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2025/12/13/tourism-power-and-dependency-the-case-for-a-mercantilist-gambia/).

A 2022 UN Conference on Trade and Development report found that a majority of people visiting The Gambia booked their trips as packages. Even during peak season, local hotels only capture up to 33% of tourist spend.

These are just three examples. Multiply this globally and you have a massive problem, where the tourism industry and tourist expectation (intentionally and unintentionally) combine into an unsustainable force.

## What you can do to minimise leakage

This advice is based on the assumption that, like us, you think that the money you spend in a destination should stay in that destination rather than being siphoned out.

I’m keen to stress that tourists are not the primary reason why tourism leakage occurs and that the responsibility to fix this doesn’t necessarily belong to you.

Some governments are working to address the issue. [The Tourism Authority of Thailand](https://www.tatnews.org/) for example has achieved global recognition for its Community-Based Tourism frameworks that hands some power back to local communities with an aim of bringing about a more equitable distribution of revenue.

Government policy is likely to play a significant factor in curbing leakage. This though, will take some time to bring about, so there’re things that you can do as a tourist in the meantime.

[Susanna Shankar from Curiosity Saves Travel](https://curiositysavestravel.com/) has an extensive and thoroughly researched [article on Tourism Leakage](https://curiositysavestravel.com/prevent-tourism-leakage-negative-impact/), which includes a number of things tourists can do to reduce leakage.

Here are some of her suggestions that you can employ on your next trip.

### Support local

You can book and support local and small tour operators and businesses. Seek out locally owned restaurants serving seasonal local fare. Hire local tour guides. Stay in locally owned accommodation.

### Avoid foreign-owned, all-inclusive resorts

Find a locally owned boutique accommodation or resort – or better yet, find those non-star-rated hotels or seek out homestays for authentic experiences that prevent excess leakage.

### Small-group tours

If you don’t want to travel alone, try booking a small group tour as an alternative to international mega travel corporations. Small groups are a great way to travel and meet others without the large, invasive cruise ships or tour buses.

**Read more:** [What is Tourism Leakage? How to Make Your Tourism Dollars Matter](https://curiositysavestravel.com/prevent-tourism-leakage-negative-impact/)

Big businesses make decisions based on how much money they can make. That means there is power in the decisions that you make as a tourist. Some may try to convince you otherwise, that your choices and actions are insignificant, but that simply isn’t the case.

![A photo of Susanna Shankar from Curiosity Saves Travel](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnxl7wsnx/image/upload/v1784635623/susanna-shankar_ysd78b.avif)

Susanna goes on to say, “If you’re feeling powerless against big businesses, I always say that in this global economy, individual consumers hold way more power than we're led to believe. 

“How you choose to spend your money matters greatly, from the big decisions to the small daily purchases. Simply choosing a restaurant that serves local food will uplift local agricultural communities, buying a local beverage over a Coca-Cola helps support local producers, and buying your souvenir from a local artisan preserves a high-paying local craft.

“There are times it is unavoidable, but do what you can where you can. When we choose to support value chains, it helps spread the economic benefits of tourism beyond tourism, empowering communities to financially manage some of tourism’s impacts and build more sustainable capacity on their terms.”

Our hope is that if enough tourists make more thoughtful choices, that might influence businesses that are responsible for tourism leakage to change their ways. On a smaller scale however, you can at least do what you can to ensure that your money benefits the places that you visit.
